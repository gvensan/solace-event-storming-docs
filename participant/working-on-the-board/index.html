Working on the board

As a participant you place stickies, vote when voting is open, chat, and follow along when someone is presenting. This page covers the gestures you will use most.

The core gestures

Section titled “The core gestures”

The left tool rail holds your palette and personal tools. It starts collapsed, so the wall gets the screen; click its edge to unfold it, and your choice is remembered in that browser.

  • Create: pick a kind in the palette (the color swatches on the left tool rail), then double-click the wall. The sticky is born where you clicked (if the spot is taken, the new sticky politely nudges itself to free space; the one already there never moves).
  • Write or edit: double-click a sticky, type, and press Enter to commit (Esc cancels).
  • Move: drag a sticky (in the plain and lane views).
  • Vote: hover a sticky and click the vote control. One vote per person per sticky, and you can retract it. Voting opens at the storming phase.
  • Park a hotspot: create a red hotspot sticky next to any disagreement. This is often the most valuable thing you can do. When the facilitator later records the room’s decision on it, the hotspot settles - muted with a ✓, the question still on the wall but answered; the decision shows in its side panel and lands in the workbook’s Hotspots & Decisions sheet.
  • Delete: select your own sticky and press delete. Undo restores it.
  • Undo and redo (↩/↪, top of the left rail): step back your own last action (and forward again). Undo never rewrites history - it adds a correcting entry, so the record stays honest - and the room sees a brief notice (”… undid: move sticky”), so a sticky snapping back reads as a correction, not a glitch.
  • Ask what a sticky is: select a sticky and open its ? guide for a quick explainer of that kind, what it carries, and what it could still carry.
  • See its story: open a sticky’s history (🕘) for the full record of who changed what, with before and after values.

Speed beats polish during the storm. Duplicates are welcome, and “wrong” is fine; silence is the only failure.

The left rail

Section titled “The left rail”

Everything in the rail is yours: it changes what you see and what you are about to create, and none of it is broadcast. It starts collapsed to icons - the wall is the point - and the « at the bottom expands it to names.

✨ Auto-layoutFacilitator only, and it does change the wall for everyone: it re-packs each context into its boundary and collects everything unassigned into a band below, grouped by kind. It asks first, and the moves are logged like any other

The palette and the two link toggles are always available, whatever the phase - a hint never disables a control you can still legitimately use.

Moving around

Section titled “Moving around”

The wall is much bigger than your screen:

  • Pan by dragging the wall, zoom with the scroll wheel or a trackpad pinch.
  • Fit the whole board with the f key or the ⛶ button on the zoom bar at the bottom-right (which also carries zoom in, zoom out, the current percentage, and 1:1).
  • Jump by clicking anywhere on the minimap, top-right of the wall.
  • Your camera is yours alone; when you enter a board it fits to the content so you land on the stickies, not on empty wall.

The full gesture and shortcut list is in Shortcuts and gestures.

On a moderated board

Section titled “On a moderated board”

If the facilitator has turned on moderated mode, your stickies do not land on the wall directly. They go to the facilitator’s tray, and the facilitator accepts, edits, or rejects each one (a rejection comes with a reason you can see). This is normal for larger rooms.

Connecting stickies

Section titled “Connecting stickies”

Links carry causality - command causes event, event triggers policy, policy issues the next command - and there are two ways to draw one:

  • Live preview: while you are connecting, a dashed animated line follows your cursor from the source sticky. Point at a candidate and it answers before you click: green means the link will be created, amber means the arrow will flip the legal way round, and red explains why that pair cannot link - so a refusal never comes as a surprise.
  • The ↑ / ↓ arrows: select a sticky and two arrows appear, one above it and one below. Press ↓ to answer “what does this lead to?” then click the sticky it leads to; press ↑ to answer “what leads into this?” then click the sticky it comes from. Esc or a click on empty wall cancels.
  • Alt-click: click the first sticky (the source), then alt-click (⌥-click on Mac) the target.

Either way the grammar decides what you meant: valid pairs create a typed link, reversed pairs are flipped automatically with a notice, and unsupported pairs teach instead (linking two events, for example, suggests the policy that belongs between them). Arrows appear for everyone as soon as a board has any links; actors and systems are never wired - they are assigned (see the two layers). The complete table of which pairs connect, which get corrected, and why each refusal exists is in What connects to what.

Views are yours

Section titled “Views are yours”

You can switch between views whenever you like. Switching a view changes only what you see; it does not move anything for anyone else. The available views:

  • Plain is where you land and the working wall: everything works, including moving stickies and drawing links.
  • Lane views (actor, system, and context) arrange the same wall into lanes. Actor and system lanes are led by the cast sticky that names them, pivotal events form a spine across the top, and hotspots gather in a parking lot along the bottom. Stickies missing the grouping attribute fall into a visible Unassigned lane. Those three collected bands - Unassigned, the cross-context cast, and the parking lot - hold stickies whose position along the timeline means nothing, so they are gathered into a tidy block at the right end of their band, grouped by kind, rather than scattered across the wall’s full width.

The one exception to “views are yours” is presenting: when someone is presenting, your view follows theirs and the rest of the board is locked until they finish. Chat and the members list stay available throughout.

The ⚙ settings drawer is also worth knowing: you land on Style (wall theme and sticky font, yours alone to change), and the Board and Broker tabs show you the session’s own settings - the sticky-creation mode, whether the workshop runs to a clock and its timeboxes, the board’s lifecycle, the event-topic roots - read-only, since those act on the whole room and the facilitator drives them. If a board’s title carries a 🔒, clicking it opens the drawer at the lifecycle section, which explains what the lock means for you.

If you land on the Big Picture map

Section titled “If you land on the Big Picture map”

A workshop has one map and a board per domain, and the rail across the top moves you between them: the ⊞ chip goes back to the map, the other chips are the domain boards. A 🔒 on a chip means that wall is locked - useful to know before you go there.

The map works the same way with three differences you will notice at once:

  • A smaller palette. Events, actors, systems, hotspots, plus two map-only cards - Domain (chalk) and Team (slate). Commands, policies, aggregates and read models are not offered here; they belong to a domain’s own board.
  • No links. Instead of wiring things, you seat them: switch to the domains or teams view and drag an event or system into the lane of whoever should own it. Drag it back to the dock to un-seat it.
  • No phases. There is no phase chip or timer on a map, because collecting the landscape is not a five-act structure.

Your dot votes on the map go on Domain cards, and they are about sequence: which slice the room storms next.

Reading the wall

Section titled “Reading the wall”

A few local overlays help you make sense of a busy wall (they change only your view):

  • An event’s parties: from the event-architecture phase on, selecting an event shows who produces it, what causes it, and who consumes it in the side panel - read straight from the links the room accepted. If it says nobody consumes this event yet, that is worth saying out loud.
  • Kind spotlight: click a kind in the palette to spotlight every sticky of that kind and dim the rest; click it again to clear. While a spotlight is on you are inspecting, not editing (double-clicking the wall still creates a new sticky of that kind).
  • Cast links (⚙⇝): toggle to show which actor issues each command and which system emits each event. Select a sticky to highlight its cast links and dim the rest - a click on empty wall puts everything back. Hovering a cast link or a flow link names the relationship in a tooltip; clicking one opens a small card with the details of both connected stickies. The card follows your mouse’s attention: it stays while you are on the line or the card itself, and slips away shortly after you leave (or on Esc, or a click elsewhere). Who does what is the full account of what each assignment means and what it becomes in Event Portal.
  • Walk the story (⟲): select an event and step along its causal chain in either direction - ”← what made this possible?” and ”→ what does this set in motion?” - with the chain lit and the rest dimmed. Backwards, a sound story completes at an actor; forwards it ends at an outcome. Every break along the way is worth a hotspot. Local to you; when a presenter walks, everyone walks with them.
  • Origin (◷ origin): see every sticky where it was first placed, straight from the board’s history, then switch back to the view you came from. It is a look back rather than a working wall: you cannot drag stickies or draw links there. Note that it shows every sticky at its own birthplace at once, which is not how the wall looked at any one moment - for that, use playback.
  • Timeline (⧗, appears beside ◷ while the lens is on): arrange the stickies left to right in the order they were created, then toggle back to their original placements.

Chatting and glossary

Section titled “Chatting and glossary”

Chat and voice notes are always open - see Chat, voice notes, and presence. You can also add terms to the shared glossary: select text in a sticky and choose “Add to glossary”, or open the glossary dock directly.
